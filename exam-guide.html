Sample: Interresponse Time (A.1) Study guide preview

Interresponse time (IRT) is the elapsed time between the end of one response and the beginning of the next response of the same type. It is a continuous measurement procedure.

Example: A client engages in hand-flapping. The flapping ends at 10:02:15, and the next instance begins at 10:03:40 â€” the IRT for that pair is 1 minute 25 seconds.

How it differs from latency: Latency measures the time from a specific antecedent (like an instruction) to the start of a response. IRT measures the gap between two consecutive responses, with no antecedent as the reference point.

The full study guide includes IRT recording sheets, common exam traps, and how to choose among IRT, latency, and frequency for a given scenario.

Sample: Chaining (C.5) Study guide preview

Chaining teaches a multi-step behavior (a "behavior chain") by linking individual responses together. A task analysis breaks the chain into discrete steps.

TypeStart hereKey feature
Forward chainingStep 1Teach steps in order; prompt remaining steps until each is mastered
Backward chainingLast stepLearner always finishes the chain, earning the natural reinforcer every trial
Total task presentationAll stepsAttempt every step each trial; prompts are provided only where needed

Exam tip: Backward chaining is often preferred for learners who need immediate and frequent reinforcement, because they contact the terminal reinforcer on every single trial. Forward chaining is useful when early steps are prerequisites for later ones.

Sample: Positive & Negative Punishment (D.5) Study guide preview

Punishment is any consequence that decreases the future frequency of a behavior. Positive and negative describe what happens to the environment â€” not whether the procedure is "good" or "bad."

TypeWhat happensBehavior doesExample
Positive punishment (P+)Something is addedDecreasesA reprimand follows aggression; aggression decreases
Negative punishment (Pâˆ’)Something is removedDecreasesTokens are taken away after non-compliance; non-compliance decreases

Memory trick: "Positive" = plus sign (+) = something is added to the environment. "Negative" = minus sign (âˆ’) = something is removed. The same rule applies to reinforcement. If the behavior increases, it's reinforcement. If it decreases, it's punishment.

Common exam trap: "Negative" does not mean unpleasant. Negative punishment removes something the learner likes (tokens, attention, preferred items). Negative reinforcement removes something the learner dislikes (demands, a loud noise, discomfort).

Study tips that actually work

  • Study by weight. Sections C and D make up 44% of the exam together. Give them the most time.
  • Learn the â€œwhyâ€. Read every explanation, even for questions you got right. Scenario questions test understanding, not memorization.
  • Know the vocabulary cold. Positive vs. negative, reinforcement vs. punishment, and DRA vs. DRI vs. DRO appear again and again.
  • Practice under time pressure. Use Exam mode to get comfortable at a little over one minute per question.
  • Think like an RBT. When in doubt, the best answer usually involves following the plan, protecting the client, and checking with your supervisor.
  • Space it out. Short daily sessions beat one long cram session.
